A map of Paris, c. 1550

Around 1550 two printmakers in the rue Montorgueil cut a picture of Paris into eight blocks of wood: every church, college, gate and bridge, and hundreds of street names. It was made to hang on a wall and probably sold in large numbers. One copy survives, because a Swiss collector took it home, and it lay forgotten in Basel for three centuries.

The map

The title is printed on a twisting ribbon at the top: La Ville, Cité et Université de Paris, the three parts of the city. The sheet measures about 133 cm across and 96 cm high, and it was printed from eight woodblocks, so eight sheets of paper pasted together [1][3]. The Basel copy is coloured by hand: “richly, too richly”, as the first scholar to see it put it [1].

It is not a plan in the modern sense but a bird's-eye view: Paris seen from high above the west, looking east. East is at the top (the four winds blow from the corners and name the directions), north on the left. The important buildings are drawn standing up, with some care for what they really looked like; ordinary houses are rows of near-identical little cottages [1].

The artists bent the world to fit the frame. Below Paris the Seine turns a sharp right angle so that both banks can be shown as far as Auteuil and Passy; at the top the horizon drops so that the mouth of the Marne and the villages of Vincennes, Conflans, Vitry, Villejuif and Bicêtre squeeze in [1]. Street names are cut into the spaces between the houses and, as Jules Cousin complained in 1875, “swarm with mistakes”; the titles and legends, set in type in large cartouches, are far more correct [1]. Across the bottom runs a poem of 108 lines in praise of Paris (read it in full, with a translation), and a third cartouche begins Icy est le vray pourtraict naturel de la ville, cité, université et faubourgs de Paris: “here is the true and natural portrait…” [1]. The cartouche promises 287 streets and alleys, 104 churches and monasteries and 49 colleges; this site lists the 457 places that carry a readable name.

Who made it

The third cartouche ends with the only signature: A Paris, par Olivier Truschet et Germain Hoyau, demourans en la rue de Montorgueil, au chef Sainct-Denys: “at Paris, by Olivier Truschet and Germain Hoyau, living in the rue Montorgueil, at the sign of the Head of Saint Denis” [1]. These are the first publishers' names found on any map of Paris. They were not booksellers; Cousin thought par (“by”) meant they also cut the blocks themselves, since the rather rough cutting looks like the work of “a skilled craftsman rather than a true artist” [1]. Later writers split the work: French Wikipedia and the Institut Paris Région make Truschet the draughtsman and Hoyau the engraver [4][5]; at least one other source has it the other way round. Nothing on the sheet says.

The rue Montorgueil was a street of woodcut makers. Around 1572 Germain Hoyau was signing religious prints there with a new partner, Mathurin Nicolas, at the sign of the Good Shepherd (Bon Pasteur); the house at the Head of Saint Denis had passed to Nicolas Prévost, and the neighbours were image-makers too: the Chessboard, the Deer's Horn, the Stag's Horn [1]. Notarial records show Hoyau buying a share of the Bon Pasteur house in 1563 and 1566; Truschet died in about 1561–1564, though the archbishop's rent roll was still listing his name in 1575 [9]. Where exactly their own shop stood is still an open question (below).

A third name is hidden in the poem. Its opening stanza of fourteen lines ends at a printer's ornament; read the first letters of those lines downwards and they spell GILLES CORROSET: Gilles Corrozet (1510–1568), bookseller at the Palais and historian of Paris, whose Antiquités de Paris came out in 1550. It is no secret code: in Corrozet's own Fleur des antiquités de Paris (1532) the same stanza stands under the heading “Le nom et surnom de l'aucteur”, the author's name and surname [14]. The map leaves the heading out. Jules Cousin was the first to point the name out on the map, in 1875, the year after it came to light [1]. There is nothing else of the kind: we checked the rest of the poem and the publishers' notice for other acrostics and found none (see the texts on the map).

Cousin also recognised the whole poem: it is Corrozet's “Praises of Paris” (Louenges de Paris), which follows the acrostic near the end of the Fleur, a book Corrozet himself later dismissed as “full of errors and fables” [1]. We have compared the two line by line. The map prints the poem almost word for word, but leaves out four lines and changes a few facts: the book's eleven gates become fourteen, its thick walls high ramparts, and its hundred councillors of the Parlement six score [14]. From the acrostic Cousin concluded that the map was published under Corrozet's patronage, as a companion to his guidebook, and added, “without fear of being wrong”, that book and map were sold in his shop at the Palais [1]. That is an inference, not a document: the poem proves only that the verses are Corrozet's, and they had been in print since 1532; the sheet names only Truschet and Hoyau. What supports it is the Basel copy itself, bought in Paris together with a copy of the Antiquités (see below). French Wikipedia goes further and speaks of a commission from “a certain Corrozet” dated 1553 [4]. A note there identifies him as Gilles Corrozet the publisher, and the sentence cites a modern study, Françoise Vergneault-Belmont's Lire l'espace, penser la carte (2008), p. 168 — not a document of the time.

Where was the shop?

“In the rue Montorgueil”, but not quite today's rue Montorgueil. In 1550 the stretch south of the rue Mauconseil was a separate street, the rue au Comte-d'Artois, and the map names it so (“R. Conte Dartois”) [2]; it became part of the rue Montorgueil only in 1792 [17]. To the north the name still took in today's rue des Petits-Carreaux, which became a street of its own only in the 17th century [17]. So the shop at the Head of Saint Denis stood somewhere between the rue Mauconseil and the wall of Charles V, whose ditch today's rue d'Aboukir follows: on today's rue Montorgueil or rue des Petits-Carreaux.

The house stayed in the trade: by about 1572 it belonged to Nicolas Prévost, in a street Cousin calls a whole colony of image-makers [1]. The street's image-makers and what they published are catalogued by Séverine Lepape [15]; the house of the Good Shepherd and its owners have been studied by Marianne Grivel [16].

Which house it was, we do not know. We have found no study that ties the Head of Saint Denis, or any of its neighbours' signs, to a house standing today, and we will not guess a street number. It could be found. The Good Shepherd house, at least, paid ground rent to the archbishop of Paris [9], and the archbishop's rent rolls list houses street by street with their signs and owners; followed forward from register to register they lead to the parcel plans of the late 18th century, published by Armand Brette [18]. Deeds of sale, which name the neighbours on either side, can do the same. That is work for the Archives nationales. Our notes and leads: research notes. If you know more, please tell us.

When

The sheet has no date. Cousin thought the publishers left it off on purpose, so that the map would look new for as long as possible [1]. It has to be dated from what it shows, and Cousin did it like a detective:

  • The crescents of Henri II put it between 1547 and 1559.
  • Built by 1550: the Pont Saint-Michel with its double row of new houses (the bridge fell in 1547 and was rebuilt), the new royal Arsenal of 1549, the newly opened Porte de Nesle, and the new quay at the Port au Foin by the Grève.
  • Not yet built: the wide bastioned moat between the Arsenal and the Bastille, begun in 1552; the uniform houses put up on the Petit-Pont in 1552 (the old uneven ones are still drawn); the Marché Neuf.
  • A party leftover: the Ponceau fountain in the rue Saint-Denis still carries the remains of the decorations put up for Henri II's entry into Paris on 16 June 1549. Cousin doubted that flimsy festival scenery would last more than a winter or two.

That gave him 1551 or 1552 [1], the date Basel University Library still uses in its catalogue [3].

Two buildings seem to contradict it. The Louvre is drawn as the old castle of Philippe Auguste, with its towers and its central keep, although the keep was pulled down in 1529 and the new Renaissance wings were going up. Yet the new Hôtel de Ville is drawn half-built: one storey high, with only its corner pavilion rising above [1]. Cousin's explanation: in 1550 both were building sites hidden by scaffolding, so the artist left them blank; the woodcutters later filled the gaps, the Louvre with the old façade everyone knew, the Hôtel de Ville with what they could see [1].

Not everyone accepts Cousin's date. Sources today give anything from 1550 to 1553: “c. 1550” (Basel's open-data page and Wikimedia Commons [8]), “c. 1550–1552” (Institut Paris Région [5]), “1552 for the view, 1553 for the edition” (French Wikipedia [4]), and about 1553 in Jean Boutier's catalogue of the plans of Paris [6]. The difference matters little: the map shows Paris in the first years of Henri II. This site says c. 1550.

Cousin also argued that the map is a reduced copy of an official survey made after Henri II's letters of 8 September 1550, which ordered a “portrait” of the walls and suburbs of Paris [1]. It is an attractive idea, repeated by later historians [4], but no such survey has been found.

Paris in 1550

The title names the three parts of the city, and the map shows them clearly. La Ville, the town, is the right bank: trade, the Halles, the Grève with the Hôtel de Ville, the river port. La Cité is the island in the middle with Notre-Dame and the Palais, the seat of the courts. L'Université is the left bank, packed with colleges and convents: the map names 33 of the 49 colleges it boasts.

A wall still closed the city. On the right bank it was the wall of Charles V, of the late 14th century, with its gates (Saint-Antoine by the Bastille, Saint-Denis, Saint-Honoré…); on the left bank the older wall of Philippe Auguste, of around 1200. Philippe Auguste's wall on the right bank had become useless and most of it had been demolished under François I; the map leaves out even the stretches still standing among the houses, except near the Louvre [1]. Beyond the gates lie faubourgs, fields, windmills and villages that are now central Paris: Saint-Germain-des-Prés still has its abbey walls and meadows.

It was a city on the move. Henri II had entered Paris in state in 1549; new quarters were being laid out on the old royal grounds of the Hôtel Saint-Pol and the Culture Sainte-Catherine; the Louvre and the Hôtel de Ville were being rebuilt [1]. Paris was among the largest cities in Europe, with a few hundred thousand people; figures for the 1550s are estimates and vary widely. Of the 457 places named on the map, this site finds 143 still standing and 217 gone.

One copy, found in Basel

Maps like this were pinned to walls, used and thrown away; a single copy survives. In the 1870s Louis Sieber, librarian of the University of Basel, was going through old bundles of unsorted papers when he found “a very fine and very large plan of Paris of the 16th century” [1]. The year is usually given as 1874 [4]. The sheet had been folded and was worn at the corners; Sieber had the folds mended and the whole sheet mounted flat on canvas.

Sieber wrote to Léopold Delisle at the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris, who passed the question to Jules Cousin, librarian of the City of Paris. No catalogue of Paris maps mentioned the plan. Cousin, who by his own account disliked long journeys, went to Basel and recognised “an unknown plan from the heart of the 16th century” [1]. He tried to buy it for the City of Paris and failed: Basel law forbade the library to sell or exchange anything that was not a duplicate. Sieber instead had it photographed, eight plates at full size and two at a quarter, and Cousin published his study in the first volume of the new Société de l'histoire de Paris in 1875, with a table of every name on the map [1][2].

How did it get to Basel? Cousin and Sieber traced it to the Amerbach family of printers and scholars. Basilius Amerbach, son of Erasmus's friend and heir Bonifacius, bought the map in Paris in 1557 or 1558, together with a fine copy of Corrozet's Antiquités de Paris of 1550: perhaps the map-and-guide that was sold to visitors [1]. Basilius's friend Theodor Zwinger, who had studied in Paris from 1551 to 1553, later described Paris in his Methodus apodemica (1577), a guide to travelling with profit; Cousin noticed that Zwinger names exactly the places on the map, even with its misprints, translating the engraver's “hôtel de Brianne” (for Bretagne) as domus Briannensis [1]. Basel's copy of the book carries Zwinger's dedication to Amerbach. The Amerbach collections passed to the Iselin family and were bought by the University in 1661 [1][4]; the map went in with them, unnoticed. The library still keeps it (shelfmark Kartenslg AA 124) [3][10].

Relatives and copies

The map has a close relative: a smaller plan engraved on copper in four plates, about 66 × 80 cm, traditionally attributed to the architect Jacques Androuet du Cerceau. It is called the plan de Saint-Victor after the abbey library that held a copy in the 18th century, from which Dheulland made the reproduction most people know [1][11]. It covers exactly the same ground with the same liberties (the bent Seine, the lowered horizon), but differs in detail: its new quarters are further along, it shows no new Hôtel de Ville, and it keeps stretches of Philippe Auguste's wall. Cousin dated it a few years later, about 1555, and thought both were reduced from the same lost original rather than one copied from the other [1]. A third, smaller descendant appeared in Belleforest's edition of Münster's Cosmography in 1575, with the Tuileries palace added.

Truschet and Hoyau's own blocks had a second life. A later state, printed from the same blocks “modernised with a great many patches”, was in the French army's map archive (Dépôt de la Guerre) in 1875: the publishers' names removed, the royal arms changed to those of Henri IV, and the Pont-Neuf, the Tuileries and the Louvre's Grande Galerie inserted among buildings that had long gone. Adolphe Bonnardot dated it to about 1601. Cousin found it “incoherent” [1].

The Society for the History of Paris published a full-size facsimile in eight sheets from 1875, drawn by Fédor Hoffbauer under Sieber and Cousin [7]; a reduced reprint followed in 1908 [12], and a full-size colour facsimile was printed in Zürich in 1980 with a study by Jean Dérens [13]. Today the library's scan, 10 890 × 7 820 pixels, is free to use on Wikimedia Commons [8], and it is the map you see on this site.
