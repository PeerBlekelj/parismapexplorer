How the map was bent

The Truschet & Hoyau plan of Paris is a picture, not a survey. To lay it over the modern city, it has to be bent: gently in the centre, hard at the edges. This page explains how that was done, how far to trust the result, and where it goes wrong.

A picture, not a plan

The map was cut in wood around 1550 and printed from several blocks. It shows Paris as a bird would see it from high above the west, looking east: every church, house and garden is drawn standing up, and the streets between them are stretched or squeezed to make room. It is printed with east at the top. Measured against the modern city, the top of the sheet points to a bearing of about 116° (east-south-east), so north lies down and to the left.

Turning the sheet by 116° is not enough. If you fit the best single stretch to it (an affine transform: one rotation, one scale in each direction and one shear for the whole map), the sheet's right angles turn out to be 77° on the ground, and one pixel across the sheet covers about 0.36 m while one pixel up the sheet covers about 0.43 m. The engravers also gave more room to what mattered: the Île de la Cité and its churches are drawn larger than life, the fields beyond the walls smaller. No single stretch can undo that, because the distortion changes from one part of the map to the next.

Reading the labels

The source is the only known copy, in Basel University Library, scanned at 10 890 × 7 820 pixels (public domain, via Wikimedia Commons). The scan was cut into 88 overlapping tiles and every label read tile by tile by an AI model (Claude, see how this was built), then the dense areas again at twice the zoom. That gave 468 labels, and the check against Cousin's index (below) found two more that had been missed; after removing duplicates and decoration, 455 places. Every label keeps a permanent id (L0001 …) so links and corrections never break.

Each place was then identified:

  • Streets against the City of Paris registers of current and vanished street names, matched with spelling normalisation (16th-century v for u, missing accents, S. for Saint, words run together) and then checked one by one.
  • Everything else (churches, colleges, gates, mansions) against French Wikipedia and Wikidata, with every article title checked by script.

Then everything was checked against the one earlier index of this map: the table of its names that Jules Cousin published in 1875, when the Basel copy had just been rediscovered. Cousin gives each name as he corrected it, the engraver's spelling, and the sheet and square where it stands. Fitting his sheet grid to our labels lined up 285 of 313 clear matches to the exact square, which made his table usable label by label. It confirmed most identifications, settled 26 of the 29 open questions (further research settled two more), corrected four misreadings (“R. de la Venerie” is a damaged “Poterie”; “Cherro Coupt” is “C. Herro Court”, the collège d'Harcourt), and found two labels the first pass had missed (“Hu Leu” and “R. dei Son”).

The result: 143 places still standing, 19 rebuilt, 76 renamed or absorbed, 216 vanished and 1 not identified. One item is still open and listed in the open questions. Every place in Cousin's table cites the page, linked to the scan on Gallica.

Control points

A control point is a spot that can be found on both maps: a pixel on the woodcut and a latitude and longitude today. The bend is computed from these alone, so their quality decides everything. There are 46:

  • 21 drawn buildings whose site is known exactly: Notre-Dame, Saint-Eustache, the Tour Saint-Jacques, Saint-Germain-des-Prés, the Bastille and others. The point sits on the drawn building, at its foot, not on its label: labels float beside what they name, and moving the points from labels to buildings cut the typical error from 76 m to 64 m.
  • 8 bridge ends where a bridge meets the bank, taken from OpenStreetMap. Bridges are the sharpest points on the map, because the river fixes them on both sides.
  • 11 more at the label itself, where the label sits on or right against what it names.
  • 6 at the edges, added later where the fit was weakest: five street corners that have not moved since the 16th century (rue Saint-Antoine × rue Saint-Paul, rue Mouffetard × rue Daubenton, rue du Temple × rue Pastourelle and others), placed with the City of Paris street geometry, and the site of the Tour de Nesle. Three more corners were tried and dropped: each landed 200 m or more off and pulled its neighbours with it.

The spline

The bend is a thin-plate spline: picture the map printed on a thin sheet of metal, with each control point pinned to its place on the modern city. The sheet bends as little as it can while meeting the pins. Near the pins it follows them closely; between them it bends smoothly; far from all of them it falls back to the single best stretch.

The pins are slightly loose (a smoothing setting, λ = 0.2) so that one misplaced point can't drag a whole district with it. The same spline runs both ways in the app: it puts the old map's tiles onto the modern map in the overlay, and it keeps the two maps in step in the side-by-side view.

How good is it?

A spline passes through its own control points, so measuring the error at those points proves nothing. Instead each point is left out in turn, the spline is fitted to the other 45, and the left-out point is checked: how far from its real position does it land? This leave-one-out error is an honest estimate of the error anywhere else on the map.

In the centre, on the Cité and along the river, a place usually lands within a street's width or two of where it was. Towards the walls and beyond, the error grows to a few hundred metres, for two reasons: there are fewer control points out there, and the woodcut itself is looser there. The two worst points, Saint-Germain-des-Prés (195 m) and the Bastille (190 m), lie at the edge, where leaving one point out removes most of the anchoring for a whole quarter. Before the six edge points were added, the Bastille was off by 427 m and Saint-Médard by 416 m; Saint-Médard is now off by 25 m.

Mistakes caught on the way

  • A lone point far out. The Pont de Charenton is correctly placed, 3 km beyond the walls. As a control point it wrecked the fit: the spline stretched a whole compressed corner of the picture to reach it. Leaving it out cut the 90% error from 463 m to 172 m. Villages at the edge were tried as anchors too and made the centre worse, so they are left out as well.
  • Namesakes. Looking up "Popincourt" found a commune in the Oise, 92 km away, not the faubourg by the Bastille. Any identification that lands outside the map's area now stops the build.
  • Old spellings. A plain "same name?" test called renamed streets standing and the other way round, because 1550 spelling varies so much. Decisions now rest on normalised spelling plus a manual check of each street.
  • Labels as points. The first control points sat on the labels, which float beside what they name. Moving them to the drawn buildings and bridge ends was the biggest single improvement.
  • The engraver's shuffle. Between the rue des Poulies and the Louvre, about ten street names are each one street off: the name of each street was cut on its neighbour, and so on down the line (Cousin spotted it in 1875). The first pass took the names at face value; the nine affected places now say which street each label really stands on. Three more labels sit on the wrong street the same way: “rue des Paiens” on the rue des Juifs, “rue des Chats” on the rue des Déchargeurs, and “rue du Battoir” on the rue Gît-le-Cœur.
  • The engravers' own slips. Some labels are misprinted or garbled: "HOSTRE DAME" for Notre-Dame, "Bribouche" for Aubry-le-Boucher, "Cochonnerie" for the Cossonnerie. They are kept as printed and identified in the notes.

Limits

  • Positions are good to tens of metres in the centre and a few hundred at the edges. The overlay is a guide, not a survey: don't use it to settle where a wall stood to the metre.
  • The woodcut is not a measured map. Where the artists drew a street too long or a church too big, the spline can only spread the error around; it can't recover what was never measured.
  • Riverbanks won't line up: most of today's quays were built after 1550 (the quai de Gesvres in 1642, for example), so the old shoreline and the modern one rarely coincide.
  • Many features are drawn but not named. The cartouche boasts 287 streets, 104 churches and 49 colleges; about 221 streets, 91 churches and 33 colleges carry a readable label.
  • One label is not identified and some readings are uncertain; they are marked as such in the app.

How this was built

The whole project was built with Claude Code, Anthropic's AI coding assistant, working with a human editor: reading the labels off the scan, matching them to the street registers and Wikipedia, choosing and placing the control points, fitting the spline, writing the place summaries, and writing the site and this page. The human side set the direction, made the design decisions and reviewed the results. An AI reading of a 500-year-old woodcut can be wrong, which is why every uncertain reading is flagged and every identification links to its source; please report mistakes.
